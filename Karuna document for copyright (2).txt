Abstract

Urban animal rescue is constrained by poor coordination rather than limited resources. Injured animals are reported

through fragmented channels, rescue response times stretch to 24 hours or more, and no single system connects

reporting, treatment, donations, and outcomes for an individual case. This work proposes KARUNA, a unified, AI-

assisted smartphone platform to address these gaps. When a citizen photographs an injured animal, the application

(app) automatically captures GPS and timestamp data, then passes the image to a YOLOv8-based detection model fine-

tuned on Indian street animal injury types. A hybrid triage engine combining veterinary decision rules with gradient-

boosted classification produces a severity score and multilingual first-aid guidance. That severity score feeds directly

into an automated matching algorithm that dispatches the nearest qualified responder without manual intervention. All

subsequent events, including collection, treatment, discharge, donation, and adoption, are logged against a single case

record and shared with all stakeholders in real-time. Per-case donation tracking follows the transparency principles used

by blockchain-based charitable systems, while avoiding blockchain infrastructure complexity through an append-only

per-case event log. The platform is designed for multi-organisation, multi-city deployment, generating standardised,

georeferenced incident data that can directly support municipal ABC programme planning and enable citizen-driven

animal health surveillance.

1 Problem Statement

Walk through any Indian city on a Sunday morning and the odds are good that you will pass a dog or a cow lying injured

on the roadside. What happens next depends entirely on luck. If someone stops, they will likely call a number they
found online, wait, call another, and eventually give up or take matters into their own hands. There is no single number

to call. There is no guarantee anyone will come. The animal may wait hours, or it may not survive the wait at all.
This is not a resource problem. NGOs exist. Veterinary clinics exist. Volunteers exist in most cities. The problem

is that none of these actors can see each other. A rescue NGO in Vijayawada has no real-time view of which cases its
volunteers are already handling. A donor who sent money last month has no idea whether it reached the animal they saw

in a Facebook post. A municipal ABC officer planning sterilisation routes has no incident data to work from. Everyone

1



is trying, but everyone is working blind. The result is a system that is simultaneously overstretched and underused. The
same case gets reported three times while another one goes unnoticed entirely, and the animals pay for the gap with their
lives.

2 Literature Review and Gaps

The research landscape here splits into three reasonably distinct threads, though none of them quite connects to the
others. The first component is citizen-facing reporting. In [1], researchers showed that a structured web portal could
replace informal phone calls and route incidents to the right authority. This is a genuine step forward; however, the system
is limited to a web-based interface, lacks GPS and image capture capabilities, and stops at the routing stage. In [2],
researchers embedded an AI chatbot into a mobile reporting app that demonstrably reduce triage latency and lowered the
load on human dispatchers. In the deployed space, platforms like Let It Wag [3], Strayz [4], and PetFinder [5] handle basic
reporting and NGO routing, but none of them touch treatment tracking, per-case donations, or anything downstream of
the initial handoff. In the Indian context, Researchers in [6] developed HSIApps, a mobile and web dashboard for street
dog sterilisation programmes that digitises field data capture, and in [7] researchers separately modelled free-roaming
dog population management through systems dynamics; both contributions advance specific operational segments but
neither offers an integrated, citizen-facing rescue-to-outcome platform.

The second thread is computer vision for animals. In [8], researchers benchmarked a cascaded YOLOv8 system on
animal detection and reported 97% accuracy across species using adaptive preprocessing and multi-feature extraction. In
[9], researchers developed a YOLOplusUNet deep learning pipeline for automated wound detection and size estimation
in animal subjects, demonstrating reliable localisation of wound boundaries. None of these, however, were designed for
the urban stray context in India, and none feed their outputs into any kind of triage or dispatch pipeline. On the triage
methodology side, in [10], researchers Fernandes et al. demonstrated that supervised ML models can match trained
nurses on patient triage severity classification. These are human medicine papers, but the hybrid ruleplusML approach
they validate is directly applicable here.

The third thread is donation transparency and fund accountability. In [11], researchers built a sustainable blockchain
framework for the financial support of charitable organisations and showed it meaningfully improved donor confidence.
In [12], researchers went a step further by proposing a comprehensive blockchain-based charity collection framework with
smart contracts, an economic model, and CharityCoin for end-to-end audit trail. In [11], researchwers demonstrated
transparent public fund tracking using a blockchain-based charity donation service system during COVID19. Separately,
in [13], researchers used CatBoost models to predict shelter adoption timelines, which is a useful data point for how
analytics can drive animal welfare decision making.

What none of this adds up to is a complete picture of a single case. In [14], researchers are direct about this: most

digital systems for zoonotic disease surveillance are built for institutional or passive use, not for citizendriven realtime
incident management. In [15], researchers reach a similar conclusion from the animal health surveillance side; their
scoping review finds real gaps in multisector, citizen-driven data collection that current systems simply do not address.

The throughline is obvious: reporting is handled separately from treatment, treatment from donations, and donations
from any kind of research output. No one has built the thing that connects all of them for a single animal, from the

moment someone finds it on the street to whatever happens to it next.
The authors reviewed existing literature in this domain to analyse current approaches and identify the research gaps.

Field operations and programme management, (c) AI detection, triage, and prediction, (d) Financial transparency and

3 Objective

To develop a unified, AI assisted digital platform (KARUNA) that closes the current gaps in urban animal welfare by

integrating image and GPS-based citizen reporting, automated AI triage and First aid, coordinated multistakeholder
rescue and treatment workflows, and transparent, case wise donation and adoption tracking across multiple Indian cities.

4 Proposed Idea: KARUNA A Unified AI Assisted Rescue Platform

The core idea behind KARUNA is straightforward: one case record, one platform, all the way through. A citizen finds

a dog bleeding on the street. That moment the photograph, the GPS pin, the timestamp is the start of a record that
stays alive until the animal is either adopted, released, or lost. Every actor who touches that case sees the same record.

Nothing gets lost in a forwarded WhatsApp message. Nothing starts over when the animal moves from the street to a
clinic.

4.1 How the Platform Works

When a citizen photographs an injured animal, the app captures GPS and a timestamp automatically so the user does not
need to do anything extra. That image goes to an AI model that identifies the species and any visible injury indicators,
and comes back with a severity label: critical, urgent, or routine. Based on that, the app generates first aid steps in the
user’s preferred language, so the case has something concrete to do while they wait. The report becomes a structured
case record, and a matching algorithm finds the best available responder by weighing distance, current caseload, and
relevant skills, then sends a notification.

The responder accepts, gets a map link, and starts logging updates as the case moves forward collected, at clinic,
under treatment, discharged. All of this is visible to the original reporter in real time; they are not left wondering whether
anyone came. For high cost treatments, a casespecific donation page goes live automatically. Every rupee that comes in
is logged against that animal’s actual bills, not a general fund. When the animal recovers, it can be listed for adoption
on the same platform, with adopter verification and postplacement checkins built in. All of it feeds into dashboards that

municipal ABC programmes can use, including hotspot maps, response time trends, disease cluster signals, and real-time
animal health data.

4.2 Key Components and Technologies

The citizen app is built in React Native or Flutter, which means one codebase covers both Android and iOS. Camera,
GPS, offline queuing, multilingual first aid instructions these are the basics. The organisational side lives in React.js
dashboards, each scoped tightly to the user’s role: dispatchers see cases and routing, vets see referrals and treatment

records, admins see analytics and partner management. The backend runs as FastAPI or Node.js microservices with
REST and GraphQL endpoints. Gemini handles the image identification tasks that are too heavy for an ondevice model.

Auth is JWTbased, and rate limiting plus structured logging sit at the API gateway.
For data, PostgreSQL handles all relational records cases, users, treatment logs, donation transactions, adoption

history. Firebase Firestore keeps the live case state for realtime dashboard updates. Media goes to AWS S3 or Google

Cloud Storage, served via CDN. Routing is Google Maps with OSRM as a costconscious fallback. Notifications go through

Firebase Cloud Messaging, with Twilio for SMS and WhatsApp when push is unavailable.
The AI stack is where the technical investment is concentrated. The injury and species detection model is YOLOv8,

finetuned on photographs of Indian street animals covering the injury types that actually show up in urban rescue: open

wounds, suspected fractures, active bleeding, emaciation, and eye injuries. The triage engine takes those outputs and

runs them through a hybrid of veterinary decision rules and a gradientboosted ML classifier, producing a severity level
and a ranked first aid sequence. The matching engine then uses that severity level alongside GPS coordinates to score

5



candidate responders. Payments go through Razorpay, PayU, and Stripe. Every donation writes an immutable event
to a percase financial log, giving the transparency that blockchain-based systems have proven effective for charitable
accountability without the complexity of an actual blockchain [12] [11].

4.3 Why the Idea Is Unique

It is not that any single component here is unprecedented. YOLOv8 exists. Donation ledgers exist. Rescue reporting apps

exist. Platforms like Let It Wag [3], PetFinder [5], and Strayz [4] handle intake and routing; HSIApps [6] digitises field
data capture for ABC programmes; systems modelling work [7] quantifies population management strategies. What none
of these platforms do is connect image-based injury detection, automated severity-driven dispatch, treatment tracking,
and per-case donation accounting into a single workflow for one animal. KARUNA makes the severity score from the AI
triage engine a direct input into the matching algorithm. That means the urgency of the triage decision shapes who gets
dispatched and how fast, without a human dispatcher making that call in between. Based on the reviewed literature,
this direct coupling between automated image-based triage output and dispatch scoring has not been reported in any
deployed urban animal rescue platform. The per-case financial ledger is similarly specific: donors can see the animal, the
bills, and the outcome, not just a receipt confirming that money left their account. And because the platform is designed
for multi-organisation, multi-city use from the start, its data accumulates in a standardised schema that is actually useful
for the kind of epidemiological and welfare research that in [14] [15], researchers identify as urgently needed.

5 Technical Specifications

5.1 System Architecture

KARUNA runs as independently deployable microservices across four layers: client apps, API services, AI processing,
and data storage. Each layer scales on its own, so a spike in media uploads during a flood related rescue surge does not
drag down the case management API. Everything is containerised in Docker and deployed on AWS or Google Cloud with
autoscaling on.

5.2 Client Applications

The citizen app is React Native or Flutter one codebase, Android and iOS both covered. It asks for camera and GPS at
install, keeps a local offline queue for poor connectivity areas, and preloads first aid content so it works even without data.

6



The organisational dashboards are React.js SPAs. Each role gets a different view: dispatchers see the live case map and
assignment queue, vets see referrals and treatment records, and admins see platform analytics and partner management.
Nobody sees more than they need.

5.3 Backend and APIs

The API layer is FastAPI or Node.js, with both REST and GraphQL endpoints depending on what each client needs.

Gemini handles image identification calls that are too heavy for the on-device model. All traffic is HTTPS, with rate
limiting, input sanitisation, and request logging enforced at the gateway. Auth runs on JWT with role-based access
control across five user types: citizen, volunteer, NGO staff, veterinarian, and administrator.

5.4 Databases and Storage

PostgreSQL holds everything that needs to be auditable user accounts, case records, triage outputs, treatment logs,

donation transactions, adoption records. Firebase Firestore handles the live state of active cases, pushing status updates
to dashboards in real time without polling. Images and video go to AWS S3 or Google Cloud Storage and are served via
CDN, which matters a lot for response times when the platform spans multiple cities.

5.5 AI and Decision Engines

The detection model is YOLOv8, finetuned on a dataset of Indian street animal photographs tagged for the injury
presentations that actually come up in urban rescue work: open wounds, suspected fractures, active bleeding, severe
emaciation, and eye injuries. It outputs bounding boxes, a species label, and injury category tags. Those go into the
triage engine, a hybrid of a veterinary decision tree and a gradient boosted classifier trained on historical case outcomes
which produces a severity level and a ranked first aid sequence. The severity level then feeds directly into the matching
engine, which scores candidate responders on distance, specialisation, and current opencase load. That direct connection
between triage output and dispatch scoring is the design choice that makes this system different from everything else
reviewed.

5.6 Maps, Routing, and Communication

Turnbyturn routing uses Google Maps Directions, with OSRM as a cost fallback at scale. Push notifications go through
Firebase Cloud Messaging. For users without smartphones or stable data, the same alerts go via SMS and WhatsApp
through Twilio. Receipts and case closure reports go by email through Amazon SES or SendGrid.

5.7 Payments and Transparency

Razorpay and PayU handle domestic payments, Stripe for international donors. Every completed transaction writes
an immutable event to a percase financial log amount, timestamp, donor identifier, and the specific bill it offsets. The

NGO and the donor can export this log as PDF or CSV at any time. While this append-only event log does not

provide the cryptographic immutability of a blockchain, it delivers practical per-case transparency at substantially lower
infrastructure complexity.

5.8 NonFunctional Specifications

Target p95 API response time is under 500 ms for standard operations. Triage calls, which go to an external model,

should return within three to five seconds under normal load. Database and media storage scale independently of the
API and AI layers, so a photo upload surge does not slow down case management.

6 Expected Outcome

The pilot runs in Vijayawada and two nearby urban centres chosen because the NGO network there is already active and
municipal partners have indicated willingness to integrate with ABC planning data. Within year one, the targets are:

median rescue response time down from roughly 24 hours to under two hours; treatment success rate at 85% or above; at

7



least 40% of recovered animals placed in verified adoptions; and donor retention defined as a second contribution within
twelve months at 70% or above. The last target is the one most directly tied to the percase transparency model. Indian
donors do not keep giving to generic NGO accounts. They give when they can see what happened.

7 Novelty

When someone finds an injured animal, no existing app tells them what to actually do with it. Current platforms use the
photograph to route the report. KARUNA uses it to infer the injury type, classify severity, and generate specific first aid
steps in the user’s language before anyone professional has arrived. That alone is new. But the more significant novelty
is what happens next. The severity score does not just get displayed. It becomes an input variable in the matching
engine’s scoring function. Urgency drives dispatch, automatically. No human dispatcher sits between the AI assessment
and the field response. Based on the reviewed literature including deployed platforms such as Let It Wag [3], PetFinder
[5], Strayz [4], and HSIApps [6], as well as academic systems reported in [1], and [2], no urban animal rescue system has
reported this direct coupling between image-based triage and automated dispatch scoring.

9 Target Beneficiaries

The animals come first. Everything else in this document is in service of the fact that a faster, better coordinated response
means fewer animals die waiting. NGOs and rescue groups benefit too; they stop receiving the same case through five
channels and start seeing a clean intake queue with cases already triaged and ranked. Vets receive referrals that arrive
with images, location history, and a first aid log already attached; they can prepare before the animal walks through the
door rather than starting from zero. Citizen volunteers get something they currently lack entirely: confirmation. Right
now, if you report a case, you have no idea whether anyone came. KARUNA closes that loop.

Municipal ABC units get real incident data, not estimates. That changes how sterilisation and vaccination routes
get planned. Donors get per-case receipts with outcomes, charitable accountability shows is meaningfully better for
retention than a generic thankyou email. And researchers, epidemiologists, One Health practitioners, urban ecologists

get a structured, geographically tagged, continuously updated dataset that does not currently exist anywhere for Indian
urban street animals.

10 Legal Framework

A few legal frameworks are directly relevant and worth being precise about. On animal welfare, the Prevention of

Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960 sets the care baseline that all rescue activity on the platform must meet. The Animal
Birth Control (Dogs) Rules, 2023 are also relevant: they place responsibility for stray dog management on local bodies

and explicitly prohibit relocation. KARUNA’s ABC integration is designed to feed into those programmes, not create a
parallel structure.

On data, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 applies to everything the platform collects location records,

photographs, contact details of reporters and volunteers. The design principle is minimal collection: location is retained

only for the duration of an active case, and all research exports strip identifying information. On fundraising, NGOs
collecting donations through the platform need to be compliant with FCRA for foreign contributions and with their
Income Tax Act obligations as charitable organisations. KARUNA itself does not hold funds; money settles directly to

the NGO’s registered account through the payment gateway, with the percase log as the audit trail. On public health,

the data sharing features for municipal ABC units operate within state animal husbandry and ULB guidelines for stray
animal management and zoonotic disease reporting not outside them.
